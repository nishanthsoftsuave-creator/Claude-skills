# Why Data Synthesis
(Source: PDF pp. 727-730, from the heading "Why Data Synthesis" on PDF p. 727 onward)

## What the section covers
Five reasons teams synthesize data: to increase quantity, to increase coverage, to raise quality, to avoid privacy problems, and to distill models. The first three correspond to the "golden data trio" of quantity, coverage and quality (PDF p. 727).

## Meaning
Synthetic data appeals because it can improve the golden data trio, mitigate privacy concerns, and enable distillation (PDF p. 727).

## How it works: the five reasons

### 1. Increase quantity (PDF p. 728)
The main reason: data can be produced at scale, giving a plentiful supply for training and testing. In theory more data helps models generalize across more tasks. It is especially useful where real data is scarce or hard to get, with examples of rare weather conditions, deep-sea exploration, and accidents for self-driving cars.

### 2. Increase coverage (PDF pp. 728-729)
- You can generate data with targeted characteristics to improve performance or elicit particular behaviors: very short or very long texts, conversations containing toxic phrases for a toxicity detector, or, conversely, safe data when real data is toxic.
- AI is commonly used to synthesize adversarial examples, and to generate data for a rare class to address class imbalance.
- Example, TrueTeacher (Gekhman et al., 2022): LLMs generated factually inconsistent summaries, used to train models that detect factual inconsistency (PDF p. 728).
- Example, Anthropic's "Discovering Language Model Behaviors with Model-Written Evaluations" (Perez et al., 2022): synthesis techniques produced datasets testing 154 AI behaviors, including personality traits, political views, ethical stances and social biases. In head-to-head comparison with human-written sets, the paper reports "LM-written datasets approach the quality of human-written ones, sometimes even exceeding them" (PDF pp. 728-729).
- Summary: generate targeted data where existing data is insufficient (PDF p. 729).

### 3. Increase quality (PDF pp. 729-730)
- Though synthetic data is commonly assumed to be worse, the reverse can hold because humans have fundamental limitations. Examples: tool-use data, where humans and AI operate and prefer tools differently (discussed earlier in the book), and complex math problems, where AI can pose questions far harder than an average human expert would write.
- Some teams use AI to generate preference data. An individual human is fairly consistent, but across people ratings vary a lot, driven by mood and motivation as well as preference. AI-generated preference ratings can be much more consistent and reliable (PDF pp. 729-730).

### 4. Mitigate privacy concerns (PDF p. 730)
Synthetic data is often the only option when human data cannot be used. In healthcare, laws make training on real patient records hard or impossible, so synthetic patient records without sensitive information can be generated. In insurance, synthetic claims can replace real claims that carry sensitive personal and financial details.

### 5. Distill models (PDF p. 730)
To make a model imitate another, typically to get a cheaper and/or faster distilled model with comparable performance, you train the distilled model on data generated by the original.

## Why it matters
These are described as just five of many reasons. Because of the appeal, more models are trained on synthetic data and more synthesis techniques are being developed (PDF p. 730).
