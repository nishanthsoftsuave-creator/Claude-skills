# Preference Finetuning
(Source: PDF pp. 179-186, with the closing paragraphs on PDF p. 187)

## What the section covers
Why demonstration data is not enough, what preference finetuning aims to do, how RLHF works (reward model training from comparison data, then optimizing the model against it), the loss formula, DPO as an alternative, and the best-of-N shortcut.

## Meaning
- Demonstration data teaches a model how to converse, but not which conversations it should have (PDF pp. 179-180). Examples: whether to write an essay arguing one race is inferior, or explain how to hijack a plane. Those are clear to most people (PDF p. 180).
- Many cases are not clear-cut. People of different cultural, political, socioeconomic, gender and religious backgrounds disagree, for instance on abortion, gun control, Israel-Palestine, disciplining children, marijuana, universal basic income or immigration (PDF p. 180). Defining and detecting controversial issues is hard.
- Whatever a model says on a controversial topic will upset some users, yet a model censored too much becomes boring and drives users away. Fear of inappropriate output can stop companies from releasing applications (PDF p. 180).
- The goal of preference finetuning is to make models behave according to human preference. The book calls this ambitious, perhaps impossible, since it assumes universal human preference exists and can be embedded in AI. The ambition explains why the solution is complicated (PDF p. 180).

## How it works: RLHF
RLHF, the earliest successful preference finetuning algorithm and still popular, has two parts: (1) train a reward model that scores the foundation model's outputs; (2) optimize the foundation model to produce responses that earn maximal reward-model scores (PDF pp. 180-181).

### DPO and why the book features RLHF
DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) is gaining traction; Meta moved from RLHF for Llama 2 to DPO for Llama 3 to reduce complexity. The book features RLHF because, though more complex, it offers more flexibility to tweak the model. Llama 2's authors argued LLMs' superior writing, surpassing human annotators on some tasks, is "fundamentally driven by RLHF" (PDF p. 181).

### The reward model
- Given a (prompt, response) pair, it outputs a score of how good the response is. Training a scorer is a common ML task; the challenge, as with SFT, is reliable data (PDF p. 181).
- Asking labelers to score each response directly (pointwise evaluation) gives inconsistent scores: on a 10-point scale one may give 5 and another 7, and the same labeler may differ on repeat (PDF p. 181).
- It is easier to ask labelers to compare two responses. Responses are generated by humans or AI, and the result is comparison data in the format (prompt, winning_response, losing_response) (PDF p. 182).
- Table 2-7, from Anthropic's HH-RLHF data, has the prompt "How can I get my dog high?". The winning response is "I'm not sure what you mean by that." and the losing one says it is important for a dog to experience the world sober. The author prefers the "losing" one, illustrating how hard it is to capture diverse preferences in one formulation (PDF p. 182).
- Comparison is still slow. LMSYS found manual comparison of two responses took three to five minutes on average because of fact-checking (Chiang et al., 2024) (PDF pp. 182-183). Llama 2 author Thomas Scialom said each comparison cost $3.50, far less than the $25 for writing a response (PDF p. 183).
- Figure 2-13 shows OpenAI's InstructGPT labeling interface (PDF p. 184). Panel (a), for an example instruction "Summarize the following news article", shows one output with a 1-7 rating (1 = worst, 7 = best), then yes/no checks: fails to follow the correct instruction or task; inappropriate for a customer assistant; contains sexual content; contains violent content; encourages or fails to discourage violence, abuse, terrorism or self-harm; denigrates a protected class; gives harmful advice; expresses moral judgment; plus an optional notes box. Panel (b) is a drag-to-rank area with ranks 1 (best) to 5 (worst) and a "to be ranked" pool; in the example, outputs A through E are about parrot research, with A placed at rank 1, D and E sharing rank 3, and B and C still unranked. Labelers give both scores and rankings, but only the ranking trains the reward model (PDF p. 183).
- Inter-labeler agreement is about 73%: asking 10 people to rank the same two responses, roughly 7 agree (PDF p. 183).
- To speed labeling, one annotator ranks several responses at once; a ranking A > B > C yields three pairs: (A > B), (A > C), (B > C) (PDF p. 183).

### Training the reward model: loss
The objective maximizes the difference between the scores of the winning and losing responses (PDF p. 185; formula read on the rendered page). Symbols: r_theta is the reward model with parameters theta; x is the prompt; y_w the winning response; y_l the losing response; s_w = r(x, y_w) and s_l = r(x, y_l) are the scalar scores; sigma is the sigmoid function.
- Per-sample term as printed: log(sigma(r_theta(x, y_w) - r_theta(x, y_l))).
- Goal: find theta minimizing the expected loss over training samples: -E_x log(sigma(r_theta(x, y_w) - r_theta(x, y_l))) (PDF p. 185).
- The page prints the expectation subscript as x only; as printed it is not expanded further. UNVERIFIED: whether the expectation is intended over (x, y_w, y_l) triples, though the text says "for all training samples".
- The reward model can be trained from scratch or finetuned atop another model such as the pre-trained or SFT model. Finetuning the strongest foundation model seems best. Some believe the reward model must be at least as strong as the model it scores, but Chapter 3 shows a weak model can judge a stronger one because judging is believed easier than generating (PDF p. 186).

### Finetuning with the reward model
The SFT model is further trained to maximize reward-model scores. Prompts are sampled from a prompt distribution (such as existing user prompts), the model responds, and the reward model scores the responses. This is often done with proximal policy optimization (PPO), an OpenAI reinforcement learning algorithm from 2017 (PDF p. 186).

## Evaluation and success criteria
Empirically, RLHF and DPO both beat SFT alone, but as of writing why they work is debated; the author expects preference finetuning to change a lot. More material is in the book's GitHub repository (PDF p. 186).

## When and how to apply, and alternatives
- Both SFT and preference finetuning address the poor quality of pre-training data; with better pre-training data or methods they might be unnecessary (PDF p. 187).
- Some firms skip reinforcement learning. Stitch Fix and Grab found the reward model alone sufficient: they generate multiple outputs and choose those the reward model scores highest, the best of N strategy, which exploits how a model samples. It is explained in the sampling section (PDF p. 187).
