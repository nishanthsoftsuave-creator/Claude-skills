# Retrieval Algorithms
(Source: PDF pp. 500-519, starting at the "Retrieval Algorithms" heading on PDF p. 500; the section's last lines, the reciprocal rank fusion formula, run onto PDF p. 520 above the "Retrieval Optimization" heading)

## What the section covers
How retrieval works in a RAG system: the two families of retrieval (term-based and embedding-based), the vector-search algorithms behind semantic retrieval, a side-by-side comparison, how to evaluate retrievers, and how to combine several retrievers (hybrid search, reranking, reciprocal rank fusion).

## Meaning and framing
- Retrieval is not specific to RAG. Information retrieval is a century-old idea underlying search engines, recommender systems, log analytics and more, and many algorithms from those systems carry over to RAG (PDF pp. 500-501). Because the field is huge, the book gives only broad strokes and points to its GitHub repository for deeper resources (PDF p. 501).
- Retrieval is usually limited to one database or system, whereas search spans multiple systems; the chapter uses the two words interchangeably (PDF p. 501).
- At bottom, retrieval ranks documents by relevance to a query. Algorithms differ in how they compute the relevance score. The two mechanisms introduced first are term-based and embedding-based retrieval (PDF p. 501).

### Sparse versus dense (a terminology aside)
- The literature often splits retrievers into sparse and dense; the book instead uses term-based versus embedding-based (PDF p. 502).
- A sparse vector has mostly zeros. Term-based retrieval is called sparse because each term can be a one-hot vector (zero everywhere except one position holding 1, with vector length equal to the vocabulary size). With the dictionary {"food": 0, "banana": 1, "slug": 2}, the one-hot vectors are [1,0,0], [0,1,0] and [0,0,1] (PDF p. 502).
- A dense vector has mostly non-zero values; embedding-based retrieval is typically dense. Sparse embeddings also exist: SPLADE (Sparse Lexical and Expansion; Formal et al., 2021) takes BERT embeddings and uses regularization to push most values to 0, making embedding operations more efficient (PDF p. 502).
- The sparse/dense split would group SPLADE with term-based methods even though its operation, strengths and weaknesses resemble dense embedding retrieval far more. The term-based versus embedding-based division avoids that misclassification (PDF p. 503).

## How it works

### Term-based (lexical) retrieval
- The simplest approach is keyword matching: for the query "AI engineering", return documents containing that phrase. It has two problems (PDF p. 503):
  1. Many documents may contain the term, more than fit in context. A heuristic is to prefer documents in which the term occurs most often, on the assumption that more occurrences mean more relevance. The number of times a term occurs in a document is term frequency (TF) (PDF p. 503).
  2. A prompt may contain many terms of unequal importance. For "Easy-to-follow recipes for Vietnamese food to cook at home" (nine terms: easy-to-follow, recipes, for, vietnamese, food, to, cook, at, home) you want to weight "vietnamese" and "recipes" over "for" and "at" (PDF pp. 503-504).
- Inverse document frequency (IDF): the more documents contain a term, the less informative it is, since words like "for" and "at" appear nearly everywhere. A term's importance is therefore inversely related to the number of documents containing it. In the simple description: divide the total number of documents by the count of documents containing the term; with 10 documents and 5 containing the term, IDF = 10 / 5 = 2. A higher IDF means a more important term (PDF p. 504).
- TF-IDF combines both. Notation (read from the rendered page): t1, t2, ..., tq are the terms of query Q; f(t, D) is the frequency of term t in document D; N is the total number of documents; C(t) is the number of documents containing t. The formulas as printed are IDF(t) = log(N / C(t)) and, naively, Score(D, Q) = sum over i = 1 to q of IDF(ti) x f(ti, D) (PDF p. 504). Note the printed prose example divides without a logarithm while the formula includes the log; the book states both as printed.
- Inverted index (PDF p. 505): Elasticsearch (Shay Banon, 2010), built on Lucene, uses this data structure, a dictionary mapping each term to the documents containing it, which makes lookup by term fast. It may also store term frequency and document counts to help compute TF-IDF. Table 6-1 shows a simplified inverted index with columns term, document count, and (document index, term frequency) pairs. The legible rows: "banana", count 2, (10, 3), (5, 2); "machine", count 4, (1, 5), (10, 1), (38, 9), (42, 5); "learning", count 3, (1, 5), (38, 7), (42, 5); then an ellipsis row (PDF p. 505).
- BM25: Okapi BM25, the 25th generation of the Best Matching algorithm, was developed by Robertson et al. in the 1980s. Its scorer modifies TF-IDF and, unlike naive TF-IDF, normalizes term-frequency scores by document length, because longer documents are more likely to contain a term and so to show larger term frequencies (PDF pp. 505-506). BM25 and variants (BM25+, BM25F) remain widely used in industry and are strong baselines against which newer methods such as embedding-based retrieval are compared (PDF p. 506).
- Tokenization (PDF p. 506): breaking a query into terms. Splitting on words is simplest but can destroy multi-word terms ("hot dog" becomes "hot" and "dog", neither keeping the meaning). Mitigation: treat the most common n-grams as terms, so a frequent bigram like "hot dog" counts as one term. You may also lowercase, strip punctuation and drop stop words ("the", "and", "is"). Term-based systems often do this automatically, and classical NLP packages (NLTK, spaCy, Stanford CoreNLP) provide tokenizers.
- N-gram overlap (PDF pp. 506-507): the n-gram overlap similarity from Chapter 4 can also drive retrieval. It works best when query and documents are of similar length. If documents are far longer than the query, they are likelier to contain the query's n-grams, many documents get similarly high overlap, and relevant ones become hard to tell apart.

### Embedding-based (semantic) retrieval
- Term-based retrieval scores relevance lexically, not semantically, and as Chapter 3 noted, a text's surface form does not necessarily capture its meaning. Example: querying "transformer architecture" might return documents on the electrical device or the Transformers movie. Embedding-based retrievers rank by closeness of meaning to the query, so this is also called semantic retrieval (PDF p. 507).
- Indexing gains an extra function, converting data chunks into embeddings; the store holding them is a vector database. Querying has two steps (PDF pp. 507-508): (1) an embedding model converts the query into an embedding, using the same model that was used at indexing; (2) the retriever fetches the k chunks whose embeddings are closest to the query embedding. The value of k depends on the use case, the generative model and the query (PDF p. 508).
- Figure 6-3 (PDF p. 508) diagrams this. An "Index" box holds a splitter and an embedding model: external memory (documents, tables, chat history) goes through the splitter, then the embedding model, into a vector database of external data embeddings. At query time the user's query goes to the embedding model (step 1), the query embedding goes to the retriever (step 2) which consults the vector database, and the retrieved context relevant to the query plus the query go to the generative model (step 3) that returns the response.
- The workflow shown is simplified; real systems may add a reranker to re-rank all retrieved candidates and caches to cut latency (PDF p. 508).
- An embedding is typically a vector intended to preserve the important properties of the original data, and a bad embedding model makes embedding-based retrieval fail (PDF pp. 508-509).

### Vector databases and vector search
- A vector database stores vectors, but storing is the easy part. The hard part is vector search: given a query embedding, find the nearby vectors, which requires indexing and storing vectors so search is fast and efficient (PDF p. 509).
- Vector search is not unique to generative AI; it appears wherever embeddings are used: search, recommendation, data organization, information retrieval, clustering, fraud detection and more (PDF p. 509).
- It is framed as nearest-neighbor search. The naive solution, k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), (1) computes similarity scores between the query embedding and all database vectors, using a metric such as cosine similarity, (2) ranks all vectors by score, (3) returns the top k. It is exact but computationally heavy and slow, suited only to small datasets (PDF p. 509).
- For large datasets, approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) algorithms are used. Popular libraries: FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search; Johnson et al., 2017), Google's ScaNN (Scalable Nearest Neighbors; Sun et al., 2020), Spotify's Annoy (Bernhardsson, 2013) and Hnswlib (Hierarchical Navigable Small World; Malkov and Yashunin, 2016) (PDF p. 510).
- Most application developers will not implement vector search themselves, so the book gives a short overview to help evaluate solutions. Vector databases generally organize vectors into buckets, trees or graphs; algorithms differ in the heuristics used to make similar vectors likely to sit close together. Vectors may also be quantized (reduced precision) or made sparse, since both are cheaper to compute with. The book recommends Zilliz's series for further reading (PDF p. 510).
- Algorithms covered:
  - LSH (locality-sensitive hashing; Indyk and Motwani, 1999): versatile, works on more than vectors. It hashes similar vectors into the same buckets to speed up similarity search, trading some accuracy for efficiency. Implemented in FAISS and Annoy (PDF p. 510).
  - HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World; Malkov and Yashunin, 2016): builds a multi-layer graph in which nodes are vectors and edges link similar vectors, so nearest neighbors are found by traversing edges. The authors' implementation is open source, and it is also in FAISS and Milvus (PDF p. 511).
  - Product quantization (Jegou et al., 2011): decomposes each vector into several subvectors and reduces it to a simpler lower-dimensional representation; distances are computed on the reduced forms, which is much faster. A key component of FAISS and supported by almost all popular vector search libraries (PDF p. 511).
  - IVF (inverted file index; Sivic and Zisserman, 2003): uses K-means clustering to group similar vectors. Depending on database size, the number of clusters is typically set so that each holds on average 100 to 10,000 vectors. At query time it finds the cluster centroids closest to the query embedding, and vectors in those clusters become candidate neighbors. With product quantization, IVF forms the backbone of FAISS (PDF p. 511).
  - Annoy (Approximate Nearest Neighbors Oh Yeah; Bernhardsson, 2013): tree-based. It builds multiple binary trees, each splitting the vectors into clusters by random criteria, for example drawing a random line and dividing vectors into two branches by it. Search traverses these trees to collect candidates. Spotify open-sourced it (PDF p. 512).
  - Others mentioned by name only: Microsoft's SPTAG (Space Partition Tree And Graph) and FLANN (Fast Library for Approximate Nearest Neighbors) (PDF p. 512).
- Although vector databases became their own category with RAG, any database that can store vectors can be called one, and many traditional databases have extended or will extend to vector storage and search (PDF p. 512).

## Alternatives and trade-offs: comparing term-based and embedding-based retrieval
- Given retrieval's long history, both approaches are relatively easy to start with, each with pros and cons (PDF p. 512).
- Term-based is generally much faster in both indexing and querying: term extraction beats embedding generation, and mapping a term to its documents is cheaper than nearest-neighbor search (PDF pp. 512-513). It also works well out of the box (Elasticsearch and BM25 power many applications), but its simplicity leaves few components to tune for better performance (PDF p. 513).
- Embedding-based can be improved considerably over time and can outperform term-based. You can finetune the embedding model and the retriever, separately, together, or jointly with the generative model. A drawback: embeddings can blur keywords such as specific error codes (the book's example is EADDRNOTAVAIL (99)) or product names, making them harder to find. This can be remedied by combining embedding-based with term-based retrieval (PDF p. 513).
- Table 6-2 (PDF p. 516) compares them on three rows. Querying speed: term-based is much faster; embedding-based can be slow because of query embedding generation and vector search. Performance: term-based is typically strong out of the box but hard to improve, and can retrieve wrong documents through term ambiguity; embedding-based can outperform term-based with finetuning and allows more natural queries because it focuses on semantics rather than terms. Cost: term-based is much cheaper; embedding, vector storage and vector search solutions can be expensive.
- Cost and latency considerations (PDF pp. 515-516): whether semantic retrieval is worth it depends on how much you prioritize cost and latency, especially at query time. Much RAG latency comes from output generation, especially long outputs, so added query-embedding and vector-search latency may be small relatively but can still affect user experience. Generating embeddings costs money, a problem when data changes often and embeddings must be regenerated (the book imagines embedding 100 million documents every day). Vector storage and search queries can also be costly; a company's vector database spend being one-fifth or even half of its model API spend is not uncommon.
- Index detail versus speed (PDF p. 517): a more detailed index yields more accurate retrieval but slower, more memory-hungry indexing. The analogy is an index of potential customers: adding name, company, email, phone and interests makes people easier to find but takes longer to build and more storage. A detailed index such as HNSW gives high accuracy and fast queries but needs substantial build time and memory; a simpler one such as LSH is quicker and lighter to build but gives slower, less accurate queries.

## Evaluation and success criteria
- Retriever quality is judged by the quality of what it retrieves. Two metrics common in RAG evaluation frameworks are context precision (also called context relevance) and context recall, or just precision and recall (PDF p. 513). Context precision: of all documents retrieved, what percentage is relevant to the query. Context recall: of all documents relevant to the query, what percentage was retrieved (PDF pp. 513-514).
- To compute them, curate an evaluation set of test queries and documents, annotate each test document as relevant or not for each query (by humans or AI judges), then compute the retriever's precision and recall on that set (PDF p. 514).
- In production some RAG frameworks support only context precision, not recall, because recall requires annotating the relevance of every document in the database to a query. Precision only requires comparing retrieved documents to the query, which an AI judge can do (PDF p. 514).
- If ranking order matters (more relevant documents first), use NDCG (normalized discounted cumulative gain), MAP (Mean Average Precision) or MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank) (PDF p. 514).
- For semantic retrieval also evaluate the embeddings. As in Chapter 3, embeddings can be judged on their own (good if more similar documents have closer embeddings) or by how well they serve specific tasks. The MTEB benchmark (Muennighoff et al., 2023) evaluates embeddings across retrieval, classification, clustering and other tasks (PDF p. 515).
- A retriever should also be judged within the whole RAG system: it is good if it helps the system produce high-quality answers; generative output evaluation was covered in Chapters 3 and 4 (PDF p. 515).
- ANN-Benchmarks (PDF pp. 517-518) compares ANN algorithms across several datasets on four metrics that expose indexing versus querying trade-offs: recall (fraction of true nearest neighbors found); queries per second (QPS, crucial for high-traffic applications); build time (important if the index must be updated often, e.g. because data changes); and index size (important for scalability and storage).
- BEIR (Benchmarking IR; Thakur et al., 2021) is an evaluation harness for retrieval that supports retrieval systems across 14 common retrieval benchmarks (PDF p. 518).
- Summary the book gives: evaluate a RAG system both per component and end to end, by (1) evaluating retrieval quality, (2) evaluating the final RAG outputs, and (3) evaluating the embeddings for embedding-based retrieval (PDF p. 518).

## When and how to apply: combining retrieval algorithms
- Since algorithms have distinct strengths, production retrieval systems usually combine several. Combining term-based and embedding-based retrieval is called hybrid search (PDF p. 518).
- In sequence: a cheap, less precise retriever (for example term-based) fetches candidates, then a more precise but costlier mechanism (for example k-nearest neighbors) picks the best among them. This second step is also called reranking (PDF p. 519). Examples: for the term "transformer", fetch every document containing the word whether about the electric device, the neural architecture or the movie, then use vector search to find those truly related to your intended meaning. For "Who's responsible for the most sales to X?", first fetch all documents associated with X by keyword, then use vector search to retrieve context for "Who's responsible for the most sales?" (PDF p. 519).
- In parallel as an ensemble: several retrievers fetch candidates at the same time and their rankings are merged. The merging method is reciprocal rank fusion (RRF; Cormack et al., 2009), which scores each document by its rank in each retriever's list. Simplified: rank 1 scores 1/1 = 1, rank 2 scores 1/2 = 0.5, so higher ranks score more (PDF p. 519). A document's final score sums its scores across retrievers, so a document ranked first by one retriever and second by another gets 1 + 0.5 = 1.5. The book calls this an oversimplification (PDF p. 520).
- Actual RRF formula (read from the rendered page): Score(D) = sum over i = 1 to n of 1 / (k + r_i(D)), where n is the number of ranked lists (one per retriever), r_i(D) is the rank of document D under retriever i, and k is a constant that avoids division by zero and controls the influence of lower-ranked documents; a typical k is 60 (PDF p. 520).

## Limitations and failure modes
- Term-based: ambiguity of terms returns wrong documents; multi-word terms lost by naive tokenization; long documents inflate raw term frequency; n-gram overlap degrades when documents are much longer than queries (PDF pp. 506-507, 512-516).
- Embedding-based: a bad embedding model sinks retrieval; keywords like error codes and product names may be obscured; embedding generation, vector storage and search add cost and latency; frequent data change forces costly re-embedding (PDF pp. 508-516).
- Exact k-NN does not scale beyond small datasets (PDF p. 509).
