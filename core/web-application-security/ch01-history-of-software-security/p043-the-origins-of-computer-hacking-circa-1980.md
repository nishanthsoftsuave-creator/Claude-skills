# The Origins of Computer Hacking, Circa 1980
(Source: PDF p. 43, with the rest of the section on PDF p. 44)

## What the section covers
How the spread of personal computers in the 1980s produced the first computer viruses, the first legal consequences, and the continuity between those early attacks and browser attacks today.

## Personal computers
- Apple released the Apple 1 in 1976. It was not configured out of the box: the buyer had to supply several components and connect them to the motherboard, and only a few hundred were built and sold (PDF p. 43).
- In 1982 Commodore International released a rival, the Commodore 64, a fully configured computer with its own keyboard, audio support and multicolour display capability. It sold nearly 500,000 units a month until the early 1990s (PDF p. 43).
- From then on, personal computer sales rose year over year for decades. Computers became common in homes and businesses and took over repetitive tasks such as finances, human resources, accounting and sales (PDF p. 43).

## First viruses and worms
- In 1983 the American computer scientist Fred Cohen created the first computer virus. It could copy itself and spread from one personal computer to another by floppy disk, and it could hide inside a legitimate program, masked from anyone without source code access. He later became known as a pioneer, showing that detecting viruses among valid software with algorithms is nearly impossible (PDF p. 43).
- In 1988 another American computer scientist, Robert Morris, was the first person to deploy a virus that infected computers outside a research lab. It was called the Morris Worm, with "worm" a new term for a self-replicating virus. It reached about 15,000 network-attached computers in the first day (PDF p. 43).
- The US government stepped in for the first time to consider formal regulation of hacking. The Government Accountability Office estimated the damage at $10,000,000 (PDF p. 43). Morris received three years of probation, 400 hours of community service and a fine of $10,050, which made him the first convicted hacker in the United States (PDF p. 44).

## Same ideas in the browser era (PDF p. 44)
- Today most hackers do not write viruses that infect operating systems. They target browsers, whose sandboxing makes it hard for a website to run executable code outside the browser against the host OS without explicit user permission.
- Even so, the older patterns persist. Scalability, meaning jumping from one user to another, and camouflage, meaning hiding malicious code inside legitimate software, show up in attacks on browsers. Attacks scale through email, social media and instant messaging, and some hackers build networks of real sites to promote a single malicious one.
- Malicious code is often hidden behind a legitimate-looking interface. Phishing, the stealing of credentials, uses sites that look identical to social media or banking sites. Browser plug-ins are frequently caught stealing data, and some hackers find ways to run their own code on sites they do not own.
