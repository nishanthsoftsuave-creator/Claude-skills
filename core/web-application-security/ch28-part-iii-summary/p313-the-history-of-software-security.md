# Part III Summary: The History of Software Security
(Source: PDF pp. 313-314, ending on PDF p. 315)

## What the section covers
The opening of the book's closing chapter, which congratulates the reader on finishing all three parts and then recaps the history chapter. Chapter 28 recaps the whole book, one block per major theme.

## Chapter opening
The reader is told they now know web application recon, offensive techniques against web applications, and the defenses and best practices that cut the risk of being hacked. They should also have some background in the history of software security and the evolution of hacking, which was the foundation for the rest. What follows is a short list of the key points and lessons (PDF p. 313).

## The history recap: why it is useful
Evaluating historical events shows where modern defensive and offensive techniques come from, helps the reader see the direction software has taken, and lets lessons from the past inform new techniques on both sides (PDF p. 313).

## Telephone phreaking (PDF pp. 313-314)
- To let telephone networks scale, manual operators were replaced by automation that used sound frequencies to connect phones (PDF p. 313).
- Early hackers, called phreakers, learned to reproduce those frequencies and to abuse administrative tones that let them place calls without paying (PDF p. 313).
- The response, from scientists at Bell Labs as this summary states it, was a dual-tone frequency system that was not easy to reproduce. For a long time it eliminated or sharply reduced phreaking (PDF p. 313).
- Specialized hardware eventually appeared that could imitate the DTMF tones, which made that defense ineffective against phreakers (PDF p. 314).
- Finally, switching centers moved to digital and phreaking risk disappeared; DTMF tones stayed on modern phones for backward compatibility (PDF p. 314).

## Computer hacking (PDF p. 314)
- Personal computers already existed, but the Commodore 64 is described as the first that was friendly and cheap enough to cause a surge in adoption (PDF p. 314).
- Fred Cohen is credited with demonstrating the first virus that could copy itself and move between computers on floppy disks (PDF p. 314).
- Robert Morris is called the first recorded person to release a virus outside a research lab. The Morris Worm reached more than 15,000 networked computers within a day of release (PDF p. 314).
- The US Government Office of Accountability set official laws on hacking for the first time, and Morris became the first convicted hacker, with a 10,500 dollar fine and 400 hours of community service (PDF p. 314).
- INTERPRETATION (the module's own cross-check, not book content): the figures differ from Chapter 1 as printed. This summary says "Office of Accountability" and a fine of 10,500 dollars, while the history chapter named the Government Accountability Office and a fine of 10,050 dollars (see WAS-C01-007). Both are recorded as printed; the book does not reconcile them.

## The World Wide Web (PDF p. 314)
- Web 1.0 opened new routes for attackers to reach servers and networks.
- Web 2.0, in which users collaborate over HTTP, created a new route in the form of the browser (PDF p. 314).
- Because the web had been built on security mechanisms meant for protecting servers and networks, many users' devices and data were compromised until better mechanisms and protocols were developed (PDF p. 314).

## Modern web applications (PDF pp. 314-315)
- Browser security has risen sharply since Web 2.0. Hackers therefore now aim more at logic vulnerabilities in application code than at flaws in server software, network protocols or browsers (PDF p. 314).
- Web 2.0 also brought applications holding far more valuable data. Banking, insurance and even medicine have moved critical business functions to the web, making a winner-takes-all field with higher stakes than before (PDF p. 314).
- Since hackers now target logic flaws in source code, software developers and security experts should start collaborating, because individual contribution is no longer as valuable as before (PDF p. 315). This last bullet is printed at the top of the next page.
